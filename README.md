# W1YU Handheld Radio (HT) Guide

A guide from the **[Yale University Amateur Radio Club (W1YU)](https://w1yu.sites.yale.edu/)** for getting on the air with a handheld two-way radio (hams call it an "HT", for "handie-talkie"), starting from zero.

---

## Getting started

### 1. Get your license

To **transmit** (talk or send) on amateur ("ham") radio you need a license from the FCC, the US agency that regulates radio. Anyone can **listen** without one. The beginner license is called **Technician**.

1. **Study.** Every possible exam question is published ahead of time, so you can practice on the real questions. **[HamStudy.org](https://hamstudy.org)** is free and excellent. Most people are ready after one to three weeks of casual study. You do *not* need to know Morse code.
2. **Get an FCC Registration Number (FRN).** It's a free ID number from the FCC that you'll need at the exam. Register in the FCC's [CORES system](https://apps.fcc.gov/cores/).
3. **Sign up for an exam session.** Exams are given by teams of licensed volunteers, in person or over video. Find one with the [ARRL exam search](https://www.arrl.org/find-an-amateur-radio-license-exam-session) or the [HamStudy session finder](https://hamstudy.org/sessions).
4. **Take the test.** It's **35 multiple-choice questions**, and you need **26 correct** to pass.
5. **Pay the FCC's application fee** online when the FCC emails you after the exam. About a week later you'll receive your **call sign**, your unique on-air name (like KC1ABC). Then you can transmit. The license lasts 10 years.

**Total cost: $35 to $50.**

| Fee | Amount | Paid to |
|---|---|---|
| FCC application fee | **$35** | The FCC, online, after you pass |
| Exam session fee | **$0–15** | The volunteer team, at the exam. Sessions run through the ARRL charge $15. Sessions run through other groups, such as Laurel VEC, are often free. The session listing shows the fee. |

More about the rules: [Rules and safety](docs/02-rules-and-safety.md).

### 2. Get a radio

**Borrow one from the club**, or if none is available or you'd like your own, **order one for about $30**: the **Quansheng UV-K5 (V3)**, [Amazon ASIN B0D2QYWMNH](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0D2QYWMNH). Make sure the listing says **V3**.

Either way, **Casey (AC1GQ)** can set it up for you with the W1YU club defaults: the new software ("firmware") that adds Morse code, the recommended settings, and our preprogrammed frequencies. Or you can do it yourself by following the [setup guides](#setting-up-a-radio).

Accessories like a better antenna, or a Morse code paddle (a small two-lever key for sending Morse code), are optional. See the [shopping list](docs/08-shopping-list.md).

### 3. Get on the air!

- **Use the preprogrammed frequencies.** Your radio comes loaded with a [list of frequencies](frequencies.md) for the New Haven area, including W1YU's own club channels.
- **Talk.** Voice works great. How far you can reach depends on how your signal travels:

  | How | How far |
  |---|---|
  | **Radio to radio**, directly | A few miles |
  | **Through a repeater**, a powerful relay station on a hilltop or tower that rebroadcasts your signal | Tens of miles |
  | **Through a satellite** as it passes overhead (needs a special antenna) | Hundreds of miles |

  See [Voice](docs/03-voice.md) and [Satellites](docs/05-satellites.md).
- **Try Morse code (hams call it CW).** The radio sends real Morse code. See [Morse code (CW)](docs/04-cw.md).
- **Keep the [quick reference](docs/quick-reference.md) handy.** It's a one-page cheat sheet of the radio's buttons: changing channels, typing in a frequency, and fixing common problems.

## What can I talk about on the air?

Amateur radio frequencies are shared by everyone, and the **FCC limits what they can be used for**. When you get your license, you take on the responsibility to use them properly. The rules are in [47 CFR Part 97](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-97), mainly [§97.113, "Prohibited transmissions"](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-97/subpart-B/section-97.113) and [§97.101, "General standards"](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-97/subpart-B/section-97.101).

The main rules:

- **No money, no business.** You can't use ham radio for anything you're paid for or profit from, including anything to do with your job.
- **Don't interfere** with other operators. Don't talk over people or deliberately disrupt anyone's signal. Listen before you transmit.
- **No broadcasting.** Ham radio is for two-way conversations. You can't send out programs or announcements for an audience to tune into.
- **No music.**
- **No privacy, and no secrets.** Assume anyone can hear what you say, and that they may record it. Don't encrypt, use code words, or otherwise hide what you mean. Common ham abbreviations like "73" and "QSO" are fine, because everyone knows what they mean.
- **Nothing obscene or indecent, nothing false or deceptive,** and nothing that helps someone commit a crime.
- **Identify yourself.** Say your call sign at the end of each conversation and at least every 10 minutes during it ([§97.119](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-97/subpart-B/section-97.119)). Transmitting without identifying isn't allowed, even just to test your radio.

**OK** ✅

- Hanging out and chatting with friends
- Making plans to go out
- Studying together for a class
- Coordinating an event for a campus club, as long as everyone on the radio is licensed (or a licensed operator is right there with them)
- Talking about radio, your projects, your day: almost anything, really

**Not OK** 🚫

- Playing music, **even in the background** while you talk
- Coordinating with coworkers at a job, including a campus job
- Sending coded or encrypted messages
- Transmitting without saying your call sign
- Announcements or "shows" meant for listeners rather than a conversation
- Ads, or selling things for profit (mentioning occasionally that you have radio gear for sale or trade is allowed)

More detail: [Rules and safety](docs/02-rules-and-safety.md).

## What else can I do with this radio?

The radio can also listen to lots of radio traffic outside ham radio. These are all in the channel list:

- 🌦️ **Get broadcast weather reports** from NOAA Weather Radio, around the clock. ([channels](frequencies.md#4-weather))
- ✈️ **Listen to pilots talking to the control tower** at Tweed-New Haven Airport. ([channels](frequencies.md#aircraft-tweed-new-haven-airport-hvn))
- 🚆 **Listen in on railroad communications:** Metro-North, Amtrak and freight trains around Union Station. ([channels](frequencies.md#trains-union-station-and-the-northeast-corridor))
- ⚓ **Hear ships, tugboats and the Coast Guard** on Long Island Sound. ([channels](frequencies.md#marine-new-haven-harbor-and-long-island-sound))
- 🛰️ **Talk through satellites as they fly overhead**, including a ham radio relay on the International Space Station. This needs a special handheld antenna. ([how](docs/05-satellites.md))

## What else can I do with other radios?

A handheld radio is just the tip of the iceberg. With it you can talk a few miles radio to radio, tens of miles through a repeater, and hundreds of miles through a satellite. Amateur radio goes much further. You can:

- 🌕 **Bounce signals off the Moon** and hear your own echo come back about 2.5 seconds later. This is called "moonbounce" or EME.
- 🔧 **Build a radio from scratch** and use it to talk to people thousands of miles away.
- 🌍 **Talk around the world** by bouncing signals off the ionosphere, the electrically charged layer of the upper atmosphere, often with nothing more than a wire antenna hung from a tree.
- 💻 **Experiment with new communication technologies.** Digital modes like FT8 pull signals out from below the noise.
- 📡 **Send signals around the planet on a few thousandths of a watt** using a mode called WSPR ("whisper"), and watch on the [live WSPR map](https://www.wsprnet.org/drupal/wsprnet/map) as stations on other continents report hearing you.
- ☄️ **Bounce signals off the trails of meteors** burning up in the atmosphere.
- 🎈 **Launch a high-altitude balloon** carrying a tiny transmitter, and track it as it drifts around the world.
- 🛰️ **Download pictures directly from weather satellites** as they pass overhead.
- 🏞️ **Get on the air from parks and mountaintops** in programs like Parks on the Air and Summits on the Air, and contact others doing the same.
- 📬 **Collect postcards that confirm contacts from around the world.**
- 🧭 **Go "fox hunting":** use a direction-finding antenna to track down a hidden transmitter.

Some of these use frequencies that need the next license level up, **General**, which is another 35-question exam. Come to a [W1YU meeting](https://w1yu.sites.yale.edu/) to find out more.
